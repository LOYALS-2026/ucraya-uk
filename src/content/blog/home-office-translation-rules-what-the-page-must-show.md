The Skilled Worker guidance asks for a fully certified translation from a professional translator or translation company that the Home Office can independently verify. It must confirm it is accurate and show the date, the translator's full name and signature, and contact details. Check the page for your own route.

## Where the rule is written

The clearest wording we found is in the Home Office's [Skilled Worker caseworker guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/skilled-worker-visa-caseworker-guidance/skilled-worker-caseworker-guidance-accessible), in section 2.5, Translating documents. It is written for Home Office staff, so it tells a caseworker what to expect from an applicant. We read it on 23 November 2026, and the version we read says it was published for Home Office staff on 3 August 2026.

The page is for one route. For a family visa, gov.uk's page on the [information and evidence you must provide](https://www.gov.uk/uk-family-visa/provide-information) lists a certified translation of any document that is not in English or Welsh. We read it on 23 November 2026. It gives no detail about what the translation must show.

Other routes have their own pages, and rules change. Read the page for your own application on the day you submit, and treat this guide as a reading aid, not advice. We cannot give immigration advice.

## What the guidance lists

Section 2.5 says that when documents are not in English or Welsh, the applicant must provide a fully certified translation. It names one condition about who makes it, then four things the translation must include. That is five points in all.

1. The translator is a professional translator, or a translation company, that can be independently verified by the Home Office.
2. Confirmation that it is an accurate translation of the document.
3. The date of translation.
4. The full name and signature of the translator, or of an official from the translation company.
5. The contact details of the translator or translation company.

The section adds two more things for a caseworker. If no translation is supplied, they should request one. If the applicant still does not provide one, or the caseworker cannot verify it, the document is not accepted and the application is processed as if the document had not been provided.

## A made up cover sheet, marked against the five points

Here is a made up example to show how the five points look on a page. Both sheets below are fictional: the company, the translator and the details are invented, and neither is a real translator or a real document.

Sheet A is a made up sample that carries every point:

- Translation company: Example Translations Ltd (made up, not a real company)
- Statement: this is an accurate translation of the attached birth certificate
- Date of translation: 3 March 2026 (made up)
- Translator: Anna Example, signed (made up)
- Contact: a made up phone number and email, not real

Sheet B is a made up sample with the same layout that leaves two points out. It has the company name, the statement that the translation is accurate and the translator's full name with a signature. It has no date and no contact details.

| Point from the guidance | Sheet A | Sheet B |
|---|---|---|
| Professional translator or company that can be verified | Named company | Named company, but no contact details to check it with |
| Confirmation it is accurate | Present | Present |
| Date of translation | Present | Missing |
| Full name and signature | Present | Present |
| Contact details | Present | Missing |

Sheet B is the kind of page a caseworker could not tie to anyone. The guidance says a translation that cannot be verified is not accepted, and with no contact details there is nothing to verify. A real translation is checked against the receiving body's own rules, not against this table.

## What the pages do not say

We read both pages for the word notarised and for any list of people who may not translate. Neither page mentions notarisation. Neither page lists who may not translate a document. We say no more than that, and we do not guess at what the Home Office would think of a particular translator.

What the Skilled Worker page does say is that the translator or company must be professional and independently verifiable. A translation by someone who cannot be verified is the situation the page describes as not accepted. If a friend or relative offers to translate, ask the Home Office or a regulated adviser whether that is allowed for your route before you rely on it.

The words used in other places differ too. The pages we read for this guide use certified. Other bodies, in other countries, use notarised or other terms for their own steps. If a different body asks for one of those, read its own page. For Bulgarian paperwork going the other way, our guide to [apostille, translation or both](/blog/apostille-translation-or-both-uk-papers-for-bulgaria/) covers what we read on the Bulgarian side.

## The original and the translation

Neither page we read says whether to send the original beside the translation, a copy, or only the translation. The family visa page says you will be told how to provide your documents when you apply. Follow those instructions, and keep your originals safe whatever you send.

One habit helps in any case. Make sure the translation can be matched to the document it translates, for example by naming the document and attaching or referring to it, as the cover sheet above does. Other bodies, such as professional registers, set their own rules on originals and copies, and our guide to [certified translation of Bulgarian documents](/blog/certified-translation-of-bulgarian-documents-a-uk-guide/) compares what some of them ask.
