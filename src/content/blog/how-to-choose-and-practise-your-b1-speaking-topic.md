Pick a topic you already know well, such as your work, a place or a hobby, and prepare five short points about it, not a script. Then practise answering questions about those points. It is the first part of the Trinity B1 test, a Secure English Language Test (SELT) called Graded Examinations in Spoken English (GESE) Grade 5.

## What the topic phase asks of you

The [Trinity B1 page](https://www.trinitycollege.com/qualifications/SELT/B1-GESE-grade-5) says you choose a topic to prepare before the test and discuss it with the examiner. You complete a Topic Form in advance and bring it with you. We read the page on 23 November 2026. A second Trinity [preparation page](https://www.trinitycollege.com/qualifications/SELT/prepare-test-GESE-B1) says that if you sit the test at a Trinity centre and do not bring your completed Topic Form, you will not be able to take the test.

The blank [Topic Form](https://www.trinitycollege.com/resource/?id=3609) is a single page with a space for the title of your topic. Trinity also publishes a [sample completed form](https://www.trinitycollege.com/resource/?id=7249). In the sample we read, a title sits in the middle with five short notes around it, and each note is a short phrase, not a full sentence. The sample suggests the form is meant to hold short prompts, not a speech. For what happens in the whole ten minutes, read our guide to [what happens in the Trinity GESE Grade 5 test](/blog/trinity-gese-grade-5-what-happens-in-the-ten-minutes/).

Trinity pages change, so read the current version on the day you book. If you are unsure about a rule on topics, check the provider guidance on its website.

## Choose a topic you can ask and answer questions on

The examiner talks with you about the topic, so choose one that gives you things to say when someone asks a question. You do not need an unusual topic. You need one you know.

Use this chooser checklist. A good topic passes all three tests.

| Test | Ask yourself | If the answer is no |
|---|---|---|
| A topic I know | Do I know this well from my own life? | Choose something closer to your daily life |
| Simple sentences | Can I describe it in simple sentences, without special words? | Drop the parts that need difficult vocabulary |
| Three questions | Can I answer three questions about it, for example what, why and when? | Add a reason, a place or a story |

Below is a made up example for a fictional learner, Marta, who works shifts in a warehouse. It is an example, not a real person and not a model answer.

| Test | Marta's notes |
|---|---|
| A topic I know | Cycling to work. She does it before an early shift and after a late one. |
| Simple sentences | She can say that she cycles because the buses do not run early, and that her bike is old but still works. |
| Three questions | Why do you cycle? What is difficult in winter? What do you like about it? |

Marta's topic works because her shifts give her a reason to talk and a few good stories: dark mornings, a puncture, a quiet road. A topic with a personal reason is easier to explain than a general one, such as "the economy".

## Build five short points, not a script

A script is a trap. If you memorise a paragraph, one question can send you off the page, and you may not find your way back. Short points are easier to carry and easier to change.

Write five points, each in three to six words, in this order:

1. What the topic is
2. Why you chose it
3. A place, a person or a time
4. A problem or a surprise
5. What you plan or hope for next

For Marta, the points could be: cycling to work, saves time on shifts, the road by the canal, a flat tyre in winter, a better bike next year.

Now say each point out loud in two or three sentences. Do it without the page if you can. Each time you say it, the sentences will come out a little differently. That is fine, and it is a sign you are using your own words. Time yourself: aim for a talk that feels like a short chat, not a lecture.

Link ideas with simple words: because, but, so, when, after that. Give a reason for each point. Use the past for what happened, the present for what is true now and the future for what you plan.

## Practise follow-up questions

The second part of the topic phase is the examiner's questions. You cannot know them, so practise with questions you have not seen. Ask a friend, a family member or a tutor to pick a point from your list and ask about it.

Try these question shapes on your own topic:

- What do you like most about it?
- Why did you choose it?
- When did you start?
- How is it different from last year?
- What is difficult about it?
- What would you change?
- What would you say to a friend who wants to try it?

Answer in two or three sentences each. One word is too short, and a long speech is too long. If you miss a question, ask politely: "Sorry, could you say that again, please?" That is a normal thing to say in a conversation.

If your friend cannot think of questions, record yourself and ask the questions aloud. Listen back and note any answer that stops after five words.

## Common mistakes in topic talks

- **Reciting.** The sound of a learned text is flat, and it breaks when a question comes.
- **Choosing a topic that is too big.** "My country" is too wide. "The market in my town" is better.
- **Using words you are unsure of.** If you cannot explain a word, the examiner may ask you to, so leave it out.
- **Forgetting the form.** Complete the Topic Form before the day and keep it with your identification.
- **Practising only the talk.** Half of the phase is questions, so give the questions half of your time.
- **Going silent.** If you cannot find a word, describe it or use a simpler word. A short pause is fine.
