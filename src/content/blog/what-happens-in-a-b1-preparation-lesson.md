A B1 lesson is a 55 minute live video call in which you do most of the talking. The tutor checks your level and goal, warms you up, asks you to speak on a topic, puts follow-up questions you have not seen, then gives written feedback and homework. B1 is a level on the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR).

## The first ten minutes: level and goal

The first lesson starts with listening, not teaching. The tutor asks what you need B1 for, when you plan to take the test and how you feel about speaking. Then they ask a few easy questions to hear your level for themselves, because a level label on a form says less than two minutes of real conversation.

You can help by bringing three things: the date of your test, if you have one, a recording of yourself talking for two minutes, and the one thing you worry about most. Perhaps you stop when you cannot find a word, perhaps your answers are very short, or perhaps you understand the question but panic.

Below is a made up example of one 55 minute lesson for a learner called Ana. It is a lesson plan to show the shape of a session, not a promise of a result. Your own lesson will change with your level and your goal.

| Minutes | What happens | What Ana does |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 10 | Level and goal | Explains her goal and answers easy questions |
| 10 to 20 | Warm up | Talks about her week and her daily routine |
| 20 to 35 | Topic talk | Speaks for about two minutes on a topic she has prepared |
| 35 to 45 | Follow-up questions | Answers questions she has not seen |
| 45 to 52 | Feedback | Hears the three most useful corrections and writes them down |
| 52 to 55 | Next steps | Agrees the homework and the next lesson |

## Warm up and speaking at B1 level

The warm up is simple talk about things you know: your week, your family, your work, what you did on the weekend. It is not a test. Its job is to get your English moving, so that the harder part does not start cold.

B1 speaking means you can describe experiences, events and opinions clearly, and give short reasons for what you say. Trinity College London's page for its B1 test describes the level in similar terms, including being able to describe experiences, events, dreams, ambitions and opinions clearly. A tutor will push you gently from short answers to connected ones, for example by asking you to add a reason and an example to every answer.

Next comes the topic talk. Trinity's B1 page says its Graded Examinations in Spoken English (GESE) Grade 5 test is a 10 minute speaking and listening test with two parts: a topic you prepare in advance, and a short conversation on subject areas the examiner chooses. We read that page on 21 November 2026. The format can change, so read it again when you book. A tutor can use the same shape in a lesson, so you practise preparing a topic and then talking about it without notes.

## Listening to follow-up questions

Listening in a B1 speaking test is not a separate exercise. It is the moment when someone asks a question you did not expect and you must follow it, work out what it is asking and reply.

In the lesson, the tutor asks follow-up questions on your topic and on other subjects. They speak at a natural pace, and you practise three habits. First, you wait until the question is finished. Second, you answer the question that was asked. Third, if you did not understand, you say so politely: "Sorry, could you say that again?" or "Do you mean at home or at work?" Asking for repetition is a normal part of conversation, but check the test provider's guidance for what is allowed in the real test.

## Feedback you can write down

Feedback is the part of the lesson that makes the next one better. Near the end, the tutor stops the conversation and goes through what they noticed. The most useful feedback is short and specific: three corrections, not thirty.

A good tutor will tell you what you said and show you how to say it better. For example, they might point out that you use "since" where you need "for", that you drop the ending of verbs in the past, or that you start many answers in the same way. You write each point down in your own words, with one new example sentence.

Ask for your notes in writing or by message after the lesson, so you have something to review. If you want to see whether a tutor is right for you, our guide to [choosing an online English tutor](/blog/choosing-an-online-english-tutor-for-everyday-life/) lists the questions to ask before you pay.

## Homework between lessons

Homework for a speaking test should be speaking. A typical task is to record yourself answering two questions for two minutes, listen back and mark where you stopped. Another is to prepare a new topic and tell it aloud to a friend, a family member or your phone.

Keep homework small. Fifteen minutes on most days will help you more than two hours once a week. The tutor will start the next lesson by asking what you did, and you can bring your recording.

## How to book a first lesson

Start with the rules for your own application, because the level and the test depend on your route. The gov.uk guidance on [secure English language tests](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/prove-your-english-language-abilities-with-a-secure-english-language-test-selt) says the test must be on the approved list and taken with an approved provider, and it lists the providers. Trinity's B1 page, read on 21 November 2026, also carries a notice about a B2 Speaking and Listening test for most settlement applications from 26 March 2027, with some routes staying at B1. We have not checked the immigration rules behind that notice, so check the gov.uk guidance for your route before you book anything.
