Support differs from school to school, so the honest answer is to ask your own school. Many schools use the term EAL (English as an additional language) for pupils who speak another language at home. Help can include extra attention in class, small group work and some use of the home language. Ask what your child gets.

## What EAL support can mean

EAL describes a child's situation: another language is spoken at home and English is being added. It does not describe how bright a child is. A child can be quick at maths and still need time with the words in a science lesson.

Schools organise help in different ways. In one school there may be a named EAL teacher. In another, the class teacher and a teaching assistant do most of the work, and a senior member of staff looks after pupils with extra needs. This guide does not make any claim about what every school must provide. It cannot, because we do not know your school. Your teacher is the right person to tell you.

If you have just received a note from the school and want help reading it, our guide to [school feedback on your child's English](/blog/school-feedback-on-your-childs-english-what-it-means/) explains common phrases. This guide has a different job: it helps you walk into the first meeting knowing what to ask and what to say.

## In class help, small groups and bilingual support

When you talk to the school, you may hear some of these ideas. Treat them as things to ask about, not promises that your school offers them.

- **Help in the lesson.** An adult sits near your child, explains a word, or gives a picture or a simpler version of a task.
- **A small group.** A few children work together for a short time on speaking, reading or writing.
- **Support in the home language.** A school may let your child talk about an idea in their own language before writing it in English, or may use a bilingual book or a translation tool.
- **Pre-teaching.** Your child sees the key words for a topic before the lesson, so the lesson feels less like a surprise.
- **Seating and partners.** A child may sit next to a friendly classmate who speaks the same language, or one who is a confident English speaker.

Some of these cost the school nothing and some need staff time, which is why the answers vary. If the school says no to something, ask what it does instead.

## Questions to ask about your child's progress

Progress is easier to follow when you ask about it in plain terms. You do not need school vocabulary. These five questions are enough for a first conversation.

1. What can my child already do in English, and what is hard for them?
2. What help does my child get now, who gives it and how often?
3. How will you tell me whether the help is working?
4. When will we meet again?
5. What can I do at home that fits with what you do in school?

Ask the teacher to give an example from your child's own work. An example shows more than a general comment, such as "she is coming along nicely".

You can also ask who to contact between meetings. Many parents wait for the next formal evening, and by then a small worry has become a large one.

## What you can do at home in your own language

You do not have to speak English at home for your child to do well at school. Keep talking, reading and telling stories in the language you know best. Children learn about how stories work, how to explain and how to ask questions in any language, and they can use those skills in English.

A few easy habits help:

- Talk about the school day in whichever language comes naturally.
- Read together, in either language, for ten minutes at a time.
- Ask your child to teach you a new English word each day, and to teach you how to say it in your language.
- Watch a programme together and talk about it afterwards.

Keep it light. A child who feels watched at home may stop talking. If you are unsure which language to use for a particular task, such as homework, ask the teacher.

## Where outside lessons fit

Outside lessons are optional. They add practice and do not replace what the school has to offer. They can suit a child who needs more time to speak than a busy classroom gives, or one who wants patient help with one skill, such as writing a clear paragraph.

A parent or carer is present or nearby during the lesson. We cannot promise how fast a child will improve, because it depends on the child and on how much they practise.

Before you book lessons, check that your child has the energy for them. A tired child after a full school day may learn very little. If that is the case, talk to the teacher about a better time or a lighter week.

If you start outside lessons, tell the teacher. It takes one short email. You can say what the tutor is working on and ask the teacher to tell you if the school sees a change. The school keeps its own plan, and your tutor adds to it.

Also tell the school about anything that helps them know your child better: the languages used at home, how long your child has been learning English, and what they enjoy. These details are not secrets, and they help the teacher choose the right book or the right partner for group work.

## A meeting checklist for the first conversation

Here is a one page checklist you can copy into a notebook or your phone. Take it to the first meeting. It has five questions to ask and three things to tell the school.

| Ask the school | Tell the school |
|---|---|
| 1. What can my child do now, and what is hard? | 1. The languages we speak at home |
| 2. What help does my child get, from whom, and how often? | 2. What my child enjoys and is good at |
| 3. How will you show me whether it is working? | 3. If we start outside lessons, and what they cover |
| 4. When do we meet again? | |
| 5. What can I do at home? | |

Below is a made up example of a filled in checklist for a fictional parent, Dana. It is not a real family and not a real school. Your school will give different answers.

| Ask the school | Dana's notes |
|---|---|
| 1. What can my child do now, and what is hard? | Understands the teacher well, shy when speaking, long words in science are hard |
| 2. What help, from whom, how often? | Teaching assistant works with a small group, twice a week, in the morning |
| 3. How will you show me it is working? | A short note each half term with one example of writing |
| 4. When do we meet again? | After the next half term, the teacher will email a date |
| 5. What can I do at home? | Read together for ten minutes, talk about the day in our own language |

| Tell the school | Dana's notes |
|---|---|
| 1. Languages at home | Polish at home, English with friends |
| 2. Enjoys and is good at | Drawing, football, helps younger children |
| 3. Outside lessons | May start lessons once a week, will tell the teacher what the tutor works on |

Notice that Dana writes short notes and an actual date, not a long speech. After the meeting she sends a two line email to thank the teacher and repeat what was agreed. That makes a record you can both look at later.
