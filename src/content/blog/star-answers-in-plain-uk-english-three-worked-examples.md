Tell each story in four short steps called STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result). Use short sentences, one idea in each, and say what you did with the word "I". Plain UK English sounds confident and is easier to say aloud. Below are three made up answers, each shown long first and then in plain English.

## Why short STAR answers work when English is your second language

Long sentences are hard to plan while you are speaking. If you start a sentence with "in my capacity as", you have to remember how to finish it, and your grammar can go wrong in the middle. Short sentences let you breathe, and they let the interviewer follow you.

A STAR answer has a fixed order, so you always know what comes next. Situation: where you were and what was happening. Task: what you had to do. Action: what you did, step by step. Result: what happened, and what you learned. The three examples below are made up. They show the same story twice, with a word count for each version, so you can copy the shape for your own story. ## Question one: a time you dealt with a difficult person

A made up example from a retail supervisor.

Long and over-complicated (135 words):

> In my previous position, which I held for approximately three years at a large retail establishment, a customer who was evidently extremely dissatisfied approached me in a rather aggressive manner in order to request a refund for an item that he had purchased, despite the fact that he was unable to provide the receipt. It was my responsibility to ensure that the situation was resolved in a way that was satisfactory to both the customer and the company. I therefore invited him to a quieter area of the shop, where I listened carefully to his concerns without interruption, expressed my sincere regret for the inconvenience, and subsequently explained the refund policy while offering an exchange as an alternative. Eventually, the customer calmed down and accepted the exchange, and he thanked me before leaving the store.

Plain UK English (87 words):

> I was a shop supervisor, and a customer came to me angry. He wanted a refund but had no receipt. My job was to calm him down and follow the shop's refund policy. I took him to a quiet corner and listened without interrupting. I said I was sorry for the problem. Then I explained the policy and offered an exchange. He calmed down and chose the exchange, and he thanked me before he left. Next time I would also write a short note for my manager.

## Question two: a time you made a mistake

A made up example from a warehouse team leader.

Long and over-complicated (129 words):

> During my tenure as a team leader at a distribution warehouse, I regrettably failed to double-check a dispatch list prior to its being released, with the consequence that a pallet of goods was loaded onto the incorrect lorry. The matter was brought to my attention by a driver approximately one hour later, and it was my obligation to rectify the error as expeditiously as possible and to inform all relevant parties. I immediately contacted the driver and arranged for the pallet to be returned, notified my manager of what had occurred, and ensured that the correct goods were dispatched on a later vehicle, whereupon the customer received the order only a few hours late. Subsequently, I introduced a procedure whereby a second member of the team verifies each list.

Plain UK English (90 words):

> I was a warehouse team leader, and I made a mistake. I did not check a dispatch list, so a pallet went on the wrong lorry. A driver told me an hour later. My job was to fix it quickly and tell everyone. I called the driver and asked him to bring the pallet back. I told my manager at once. I sent the right goods on a later lorry, so the customer was only a few hours late. After that, I asked a second person to check every list.

Notice that the plain answer says clearly that the mistake was the speaker's own, and spends most of its words on what was done to fix it. That is what interviewers usually want to hear.

## Question three: a time you worked under pressure

A made up example from a teaching assistant.

Long and over-complicated (127 words):

> In my capacity as a teaching assistant at a primary school, I found myself in a situation in which the class teacher became unwell and was obliged to leave the premises at lunchtime, which meant that I was required to assume responsibility for the entire class of twenty-eight children for the whole of the afternoon until a supply teacher became available. It was necessary for me to maintain order, to deliver the planned lesson and to ensure that the pupils remained calm in spite of the unexpected change. I therefore reviewed the lesson plan swiftly, gave clear instructions to the children, divided the class into small groups, and made frequent checks on each group, whereupon the children completed the work and the afternoon passed without any difficulties.

Plain UK English (81 words):

> I was a teaching assistant. At lunchtime, the class teacher went home ill. I had to run the afternoon lesson for 28 children until a supply teacher arrived. I read the lesson plan quickly. I gave clear instructions and put the children into small groups. I visited each group often and kept my voice calm. The children finished their work, and the afternoon was calm. I learned to keep a copy of the lesson plan close, so I can start fast.

## Phrases that link the four parts, and cutting to 90 seconds

You do not need fancy linking words. A few plain phrases for each part will carry the whole answer.

| Part | Phrases to try |
|---|---|
| Situation | "I was working as..." or "At that time, we had..." |
| Task | "My job was to..." or "I had to..." |
| Action | "First, I..." then "After that, I..." then "Finally, I..." |
| Result | "In the end..." or "As a result..." or "Next time, I would..." |

The plain examples above are cut down so that you can see the shape. A real answer to fill about 90 seconds is longer, so add one real detail to the Action and one to the Result, such as a number, a name or a date. To cut an answer that is too long, remove the adjectives and the background first, then keep the Action, because the Action is the part that is about you. Record yourself, count the words, and repeat until the story fits the time.

## Practising aloud with feedback

Writing the answer is only half the job, because an answer on paper can still sound stiff when you say it. Say each story aloud three times, and ask someone to stop you when you use a word you would not normally say.
