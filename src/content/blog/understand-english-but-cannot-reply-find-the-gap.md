Understanding English and speaking it are different skills. When you listen, the words arrive and you only have to recognise them. When you answer, you must choose words, build a sentence and say it, all within seconds. Many learners are strong at the first and slow at the second. The gap can be found.

## Understanding and speaking are different skills

Think of a shop. If someone hands you a coin, you can see at once what it is. If someone asks you to hand over exactly the right coins from memory, you need more. Listening is recognising. Speaking is producing.

When you listen, you get help from the situation, the speaker's tone, the words around the one you missed and the pace the speaker sets. When you speak, none of that helps you. You must find the word, check it fits, put it in the right order and say it, while another person waits. It is normal for this to feel harder, and normal to freeze for a moment, even if you have studied English for years.

Freezing does not mean your English is bad. It often means the words you know have been met many times by your ears and very few times by your mouth.

## Three common sticking points

Most people who understand but cannot reply are stuck at one of three places. You can be stuck at more than one, and the fix is different for each.

1. **Finding words.** You know the idea you want to say, but the English word will not come. You stop, or you fall back on your first language.
2. **Putting a sentence together.** You have the words, but they will not line up. You start, change your mind halfway, and end up with a sentence you do not like.
3. **Pronunciation.** You say the sentence, but the other person asks you to repeat it, so next time you say less.

The three can look the same from outside, because in each case you go quiet. Inside, they feel different. That is why a quick self check is more useful than a general decision to "speak more".

## A two minute test you can do tonight

You need a phone, a quiet room and about three minutes. This is a self check, not an exam, and no result from it can tell you your level.

1. Open the voice recorder on your phone.
2. Answer these three questions aloud, for about 30 seconds each: "What did you do yesterday?", "What do you like about the place where you live?" and "Tell me about someone in your family."
3. Stop the recording. Do not judge yourself yet.
4. Listen to it once, with a pen. Every time you hear one of the following, put a tick in the matching column.

| What you hear | Problem | Tick |
|---|---|---|
| Long pauses, "um", or a word from your own language | Finding words | |
| Sentences that start, stop and start again | Putting a sentence together | |
| A word you can hear is not quite right, or you wish you had said it differently | Pronunciation | |

Most people find one column has far more ticks than the others. That is your first target. If all three columns are full, start with the one that stops you most often in real conversations.

You do not need to share the recording with anyone. If you want a second opinion, a tutor can listen to the same recording in a lesson.

**A made up example: Ana.** This is a made up example. Ana is a fictional learner who works in a shop in London. She understands her customers and her manager well, but when a customer asks a question she did not expect, she freezes.

She records her three answers. On playback she hears eleven long pauses in the first answer, most of them before a verb. In the second answer she starts three sentences and drops two of them. In the third she hears no pronunciation problems she can spot.

Her ticks: finding words, many; putting a sentence together, a few; pronunciation, none. So her first target is to find words faster, especially common verbs. Her plan for the next week is small: each evening she answers one question aloud for a minute, then writes down the three words she wanted and did not have, and uses them again the next day.

Ana's result is only an example, and yours will look different. The point is that three ticks can turn a vague worry into a plan.

## Why more listening alone does not fix it

When you cannot reply, the natural response is to listen to more English. That helps your ears, and it is not wasted. But it practises the skill you already have.

Speaking improves when you speak. You need to try words out, get them a little wrong, hear a better way and say it again. Silent study, films and podcasts cannot answer you, cannot ask a follow up question and cannot tell you that a sentence was unclear. Without that, it is easy to keep recognising words you still cannot use.

That does not mean you should stop listening. It means you should add live speaking. Some ways to do that are to talk to a friend, to join a conversation group, or to have lessons with a tutor, which is described below.

## How a tutor finds the gap

A tutor's first job is to listen. In a first lesson the tutor asks you simple questions, as in your recording, and notes where you pause, where a sentence breaks and where a word is hard to catch. You then see what is stopping you, because the tutor can name it.

After that the tutor can choose practice for your gap. For finding words, it might be topic vocabulary you use at once in a short talk. For building sentences, it might be repeating a pattern with new content until it feels easy. For pronunciation, it might be a handful of sounds, practised slowly and then in a sentence.

The tutor also corrects you as you speak, in a way a recording cannot. If you want to find a tutor who suits you, our guide to [choosing an online English tutor for everyday life](/blog/choosing-an-online-english-tutor-for-everyday-life/) lists what to check before you book.

## Practice that gets you speaking sooner

Here are four habits you can try this week, with or without a tutor.

- **Answer aloud.** When you read a question in a text or on a form, say your answer in English before you write it.
- **Use a bridge phrase.** Learn two or three short phrases that buy you a second, such as "Let me think" or "What I mean is". They stop the silence from feeling like failure.
- **Repeat the same story.** Tell one short story three times, each time a little smoother. Repeating helps new words feel easy to say.
- **Keep a gap list.** Each time you want a word and do not have it, write it down. Look at the list before you go to bed.
