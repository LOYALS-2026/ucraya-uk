Bring the job description to a tutor and use it as your script. Pick out the phrases the employer uses, tell one short work story for each of them, then rehearse in a mock interview where the tutor asks questions you have not seen and corrects your English as you go.

## Start with the job description: highlight eight phrases

The job description tells you what the interviewer will ask about. Print it or copy it into a document and highlight eight phrases that describe the work, such as "manage a caseload", "work as part of a team" or "deal with difficult situations".

For each phrase, write one line in your own words: "I have done this when..." If you cannot finish the sentence, that phrase is a gap to prepare for, not a reason to panic. Take the list to your first lesson. A tutor can show you which of the words are formal, which are everyday, and how an interviewer in the UK is likely to say them out loud.

## Turn your experience into STAR answers

Most UK interviews ask for examples: "Tell me about a time when you..." A STAR (Situation, Task, Action, Result) answer keeps your story in order and stops you wandering.

| Part | What you say | Plain English starter |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Where and when, in one or two sentences | "In my last job, we had..." |
| Task | What you were responsible for | "My job was to..." |
| Action | What you did, step by step | "First I... then I..." |
| Result | What changed, and what you learned | "As a result... Looking back, I would..." |

Keep each story to about one minute. Spend most of the time on the action, because that is the part about you. Use "I" for what you did and "we" only for what the team did. Say what you would do differently, because interviewers often like a candidate who can think about their own work.

## The first sixty seconds

Many interviews are decided early, by how comfortable you sound. Practise the opening on its own: the greeting, a little small talk and "Tell me about yourself".

Small talk is usually simple. "Did you find us all right?" is not a test, so answer in a friendly sentence and ask a short question back. For "Tell me about yourself", use three sentences: who you are now, what you have done that matches the job, and why you want this role. Do not tell your whole life story.

Here are twelve phrases that often come up in UK interviews, and what they mean:

| Phrase you may hear | What it is asking |
|---|---|
| "Talk me through your curriculum vitae (CV)." | Give the short story of your career |
| "What drew you to this role?" | Why do you want this job? |
| "Tell me about a time when..." | Give a STAR example |
| "How would you handle...?" | Describe what you would do |
| "What are your strengths?" | Name two and give proof |
| "Where do you see yourself in five years?" | Show you want to grow here |
| "How do you cope under pressure?" | Give a calm example |
| "Can you give me an example?" | Add detail to your last answer |
| "How do you work in a team?" | Show how you share and listen |
| "What would your colleagues say about you?" | Describe how others see you |
| "Is there anything you would like to ask us?" | Ask one or two questions |
| "When could you start?" | Say your notice period |

## Practise unexpected questions in a mock interview

Prepared stories are only half the job. The other half is the question you did not expect, so ask a friend, or your tutor, to ask you something that is not on your list, and answer without notes.

If you need time, buy it with a short phrase such as "That is a good question" followed by "let me think for a moment". It sounds calm and gives you a few seconds. If you do not understand a question, say so, or check it back: do they mean your current role? Interviewers prefer a clear question and a clear answer to a confident wrong one.

A mock interview is the most useful hour you can spend. The tutor plays the interviewer, asks questions from your job description and some you have not seen, and then goes back over what you said. The feedback usually covers three things: grammar that changes the meaning, words that sound too formal or too casual, and pronunciation that makes a word hard to catch.

Ask the tutor to record the call, or take notes, so you can listen again afterwards. Hearing yourself is uncomfortable and works well. ## A seven day practice plan

This is a made up example, a plan for Anna, a ward nurse with an interview on Friday. Her plan starts on the Saturday before. Change the details to fit your own job and your own week. It is a practice plan and cannot tell you whether an offer will follow.

| Day | What Anna does |
|---|---|
| Saturday | Highlights eight phrases in the job description and writes "I have done this when..." for each |
| Sunday | Writes her first STAR story and says it aloud twice |
| Monday | Writes a second STAR story and records it on her phone |
| Tuesday | Practises "Tell me about yourself" and the opening small talk |
| Wednesday | Writes a third STAR story, about a difficult situation, and prepares two questions to ask the employer |
| Thursday | Mock interview with a tutor, with feedback on grammar and phrasing |
| Friday | Reads her notes once, sleeps well and arrives early |

Each evening session is short, about 30 minutes. The Thursday lesson is the one that changes the plan, because it shows Anna which stories to tighten.
