It is a 10 minute, one-to-one speaking and listening conversation with an examiner, in two parts: a topic you prepare in advance, then a short conversation on two subjects the examiner chooses. It is a Secure English Language Test (SELT) for B1 on the Common European Framework of Reference (CEFR).

## Who the examiner is and how the test starts

The Graded Examinations in Spoken English (GESE) Grade 5 test is run by Trinity, not by us. Trinity's [B1 test page](https://www.trinitycollege.com/qualifications/SELT/B1-GESE-grade-5) says the test is one-to-one and is delivered with Trinity examiners remotely by video conference. If you sit it at a Trinity SELT centre, it takes place on a Trinity tablet device that a steward sets up for you.

At a centre you must show proof of identity. Trinity asks you to read its identification and security policy, and the gov.uk guidance on [secure English language tests](https://www.gov.uk/guidance/prove-your-english-language-abilities-with-a-secure-english-language-test-selt) lists the documents that are accepted. The details you use to book must match your passport or other identity document, so check the names before you pay.

Trinity's page lists the fee for its SELT GESE exams as £160, and an optional Exam Protect add-on at £35 that lets you book one retake and reschedule once. We read the page on 21 November 2026, and fees change, so confirm the figures on the [Trinity page](https://www.trinitycollege.com/qualifications/SELT/B1-GESE-grade-5) on the day you book.

One more point before you plan: the page also carries a notice about a B2 Speaking and Listening test for most settlement applications from 26 March 2027, with some routes staying at B1. The notice is Trinity's, so check the gov.uk guidance for your own route before choosing a test.

## The topic phase

The first part is a topic that you choose and prepare in advance. The page says a Topic Form must be completed before the exam and brought with you, and it includes an example of a completed form.

Choose something you can talk about in some detail without notes: a hobby, a place you know, your work, a person you admire. The best topics have a story in them and give you something to explain. Avoid a topic that needs technical words you only half know, because the examiner may ask you about them.

Practise talking about your topic for a couple of minutes, in your own words, with a beginning, a middle and an end. Do not memorise a speech. If you learn the order of your ideas and a few useful phrases, you can still sound natural when the examiner interrupts with a question.

## The conversation phase

The second part is a short conversation about two subject areas chosen by the examiner. You do not know the subjects in advance, so this is where practice with unexpected questions matters most.

The examiner asks, you answer, and the conversation moves on. Below is a made up example of how a short extract might sound, to show the pattern. It is not a real test, and a real exchange will differ. The topic here is "My town".

| Who | What is said |
|---|---|
| Examiner | Thank you. Please tell me about your topic. |
| Learner | I would like to talk about my town. I live in a small town near the coast. It is quiet, but it has a good market and a park. |
| Examiner | What do you like most about the market? |
| Learner | I like it because I can buy fresh vegetables, and I often meet my neighbours there. |
| Examiner | How is your town different from a big city? |
| Learner | Sorry, could you say that again, please? |
| Examiner | How is your town different from a big city? |
| Learner | It is smaller, and there are fewer shops, but it is more friendly and less expensive. |

Two things to notice. The learner gives a reason in each answer, not just a word or two. And the learner asks for the question again when they did not catch it, which is a normal and polite thing to do.

## Where the listening comes in

Trinity calls this a speaking and listening test, but its page describes only the two parts above. There is no separate listening section to learn. Listening is the skill you use in both parts: following what the examiner asks, working out what they want, and answering that question and not another one.

So practise listening as part of speaking. Ask a friend to ask you questions you have not seen. Notice when you start answering before the question has finished, and train yourself to wait. If you miss a word, ask politely for the question to be repeated, but do not ask every time.

## What B1 looks like in your answers

The Trinity page describes the B1 level using the CEFR. At B1 a person can understand conversations about work, school and leisure, deal with most travelling situations, write simple text on topics of interest, and describe experiences, events, dreams, ambitions and opinions clearly.

For a speaking test, the last part matters most. Aim to give an answer with a reason and an example, to link ideas with words like "because", "but" and "so", and to talk about the past and the future as well as the present. Trinity publishes preparation materials for the exam, and its marking criteria are in those materials, so read them directly and not a summary.
