Compare your letter with the case notes and ask what the reader needs to do. A C often comes from too much detail and a vague request, not only from grammar. Cut what does not serve the purpose, state the request early, and rewrite. The Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) asks for C+ in Occupational English Test (OET) writing.

## What C+ means for NMC registration

We read the [NMC page on OET](https://www.nmc.org.uk/registration/joining-the-register/english-language-requirements/accepted-english-language-tests/oet/) on 21 November 2026. It lists at least grade C+ (300 or above) for writing, and at least grade B (350 or above) for reading, listening and speaking. It also says to choose the Nursing version of the test.

So writing has the lowest line of the four parts, and many nurses still miss it. If your writing result is C, you are one grade below the line. The same NMC page covers how two sittings can be combined, including a lower minimum for writing in each sitting. Read it before you plan a resit, and check it again on the day you book, because the NMC can change it.

## The criteria the letter is marked against

OET publishes the criteria its writing marking uses on oet.com. The page did not load for us when we wrote this guide, so we have not copied the criteria names here. Open the criteria on the OET website and use its wording on the day you book.

Meanwhile, here are six plain questions of our own. They are not the official list, and they are a way to read your own letter.

1. Purpose: can the reader tell in the first lines why you are writing?
2. Case notes: did you choose the facts that serve that purpose, and leave out the rest?
3. Clarity: is every sentence short enough to read once, with no repeated points?
4. Tone: does it sound like one professional writing to another?
5. Order and layout: does it follow a sensible order, with a greeting, short paragraphs and a sign off?
6. Language: are the grammar, spelling and punctuation accurate?

## A made up weak letter, annotated

This is a made up example. Mr Brown is an invented patient, aged 72, after a hip repair, and no real patient data is used. It is an English writing exercise, not clinical advice, and a real letter must follow the task and your workplace.

Here are the made up case notes, in short form.

| Note | Detail |
|---|---|
| Patient | Mr Brown, 72, lives alone, daughter nearby |
| Operation | Planned left hip repair, discharged on day five |
| Progress | Walking with a frame, wound clean and dry |
| Home needs | Wound check and a review of how he manages at home |
| Other facts | Takes tablets for blood pressure, retired bus driver, enjoys gardening |

The weak letter, written to a community nurse:

> Dear Nurse,
>
> I am writing about Mr Brown. He is 72 years old and was a bus driver for many years and he likes gardening very much. He had his left hip repaired and he was in hospital for five days, and he is walking with a frame. He takes tablets for his blood pressure. His daughter lives near. His wound is clean and dry. I think he might need some help at home. Please see him.
>
> Yours faithfully, Nurse Lee

What a marker may notice:

- The letter does not say what the reader should do, or when. The request is the last line and it is vague.
- The job and the gardening do not help the community nurse decide anything. They are irrelevant detail.
- The blood pressure tablets are not linked to the request, so they add noise.
- Several ideas are joined with and, which makes the letter hard to scan.
- Dear Nurse is too general, and the sign off does not match the greeting.

## The same letter rewritten, with the changes explained

> Dear Community Nurse,
>
> Re: Mr Brown, 72, left hip repair
>
> I am referring Mr Brown for a wound check and a review of how he manages at home. He was discharged on day five after a planned left hip repair. I would be grateful if you could visit him within the week.
>
> He is walking with a frame and his wound is clean and dry. He lives alone, and his daughter lives nearby.
>
> Please contact me if you need more information.
>
> Yours sincerely, Nurse Lee

What changed, and why:

- The request moved to the first paragraph, with a reason and a time frame.
- The job and the hobby were cut, because they do not help the reader act.
- The tablets were cut for the same reason. Whether a detail belongs is a question for the task notes in your own test, so do not copy this choice as a rule.
- Related facts are grouped, so each paragraph has one job.
- The greeting and the sign off now match, and the tone is calm and polite.

Notice that the rewrite is shorter. A grade does not rise because a letter is longer.

## Three habits that move a letter up one grade

These are practice habits, not marking rules, and we cannot promise a grade from any habit.

1. Write the purpose sentence first. Before the letter, write one line: I am writing to ask the reader to do this, because of that. If you cannot, the notes are not yet clear to you.
2. Sort the notes in two minutes. Mark each fact as needed, maybe or not needed for the reader's action. Start the letter with the needed ones.
3. Read it as the recipient. Finish by reading the letter as the community nurse, who has not seen the notes. Can they act without a phone call?

## Practising with feedback you can act on

Writing improves with a loop: write under test conditions, get comments, rewrite, and then write a new letter on a new case. Without comments, you repeat the same habits.
