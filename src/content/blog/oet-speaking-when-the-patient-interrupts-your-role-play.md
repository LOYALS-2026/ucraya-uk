Practise with a live partner who is told to interrupt you, so you learn to stop, acknowledge, answer the new worry and return to your plan. Rehearsing alone helps with phrases, but only another person can break your flow. The Occupational English Test (OET) website holds the real test format, so read it there.

## What this guide is, and what it is not

We could not open the OET website when we wrote this guide, so we say nothing here about the format of the OET speaking test: how long it is, how many role plays there are, what the cards look like or how it is marked. The OET website is the only place to read that, and you should read it before you plan your practice.

What this guide gives you is English practice for one situation that is hard in any language: you are explaining something to a worried person and they cut across you. The role play below is fictional. There is no real patient and no real data in it, and it is an English exercise, not clinical advice.

The Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) page on OET, which we read on 23 November 2026, lists grade B (350 to 440) as the speaking score it asks for. Your own requirement may differ, so check the [NMC page on OET](https://www.nmc.org.uk/registration/joining-the-register/english-language-requirements/accepted-english-language-tests/oet/) for your route.

## A made up role play card

This is a made up example, written only for practice. You play the nurse. Your partner plays the relative and uses the interruption on the third line.

| Line | Who | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | You | Greet the relative and say who you are and why you are speaking to them |
| 2 | Relative | Asks why their mother, who is 82, has not been allowed to go home yet |
| 3 | Relative | Interrupts you in the middle of your answer: "But nobody told us anything yesterday." |
| 4 | You | Respond to the worry, then return to what you were explaining |
| 5 | Relative | Asks what happens next and when |

Setting: a medical ward. The patient is a made up woman called Mrs Hale, who is recovering after a fall. Her daughter is worried about going home arrangements. Nothing here is based on a real person.

Three ways to answer the interruption on line three, from weakest to strongest.

**Too abrupt.** "Please let me finish. I will explain everything and then you can ask questions."

The reason: it protects your plan, not the relative. They have just told you they feel ignored, and you have asked them to wait.

**Adequate.** "I am sorry you were not told. I will explain now. The doctor wants to check her walking before she goes home."

The reason: it acknowledges the problem and gives a clear answer. It is correct and polite, though it does not yet check how the relative feels.

**Strong.** "I am sorry nobody spoke to you yesterday, that must have been worrying. Let me tell you where we are now, and please stop me if anything is not clear. The doctor would like to check how Mrs Hale walks before she goes home. Does that make sense so far?"

The reason: it names the feeling, invites questions, gives one piece of information and checks understanding before moving on.

## Five habits that make an interruption harder

These are habits we see in English practice, not points from a marking scheme.

1. **Talking over the person.** You keep going at the same speed. Stop speaking, even mid-sentence.
2. **Ignoring the feeling.** You answer the facts only. A short phrase such as "I can see this is worrying" is often enough.
3. **Starting again from the beginning.** After an interruption, you repeat your whole opening. Return to the point where you stopped.
4. **Using words the relative may not know.** Medical terms and abbreviations are hard to follow when a person is anxious. Use plain words and explain a term only if you must.
5. **Never checking.** You give four pieces of information in a row. Give one, then check.

## Staying calm and checking understanding

Calm is mostly a matter of order. Use the same four steps each time: stop, acknowledge, answer, return.

- **Stop.** Finish the word you are on, then pause. A pause of two seconds feels long to you and normal to the listener.
- **Acknowledge.** "I understand." "That is a fair question." "I am sorry about that."
- **Answer.** Give the short answer to the new point first, in one or two sentences.
- **Return.** "Now, going back to what I was saying..." or "So, to finish what I was explaining..."

Slow your voice a little on the acknowledgement. A steady, lower voice is easier to follow and helps you feel steady too.

**Phrases for checking understanding.** These phrases are useful in any conversation where you explain something to a person who is worried or tired. Say them aloud until the first few come without thinking.

| Purpose | Phrase |
|---|---|
| Invite a question | "Please stop me if anything is not clear." |
| Check | "Does that make sense so far?" |
| Check in other words | "Would you like me to go through that again?" |
| Ask them to repeat | "Can I check I understood your question? You are asking about..." |
| Slow down | "Let me explain that step by step." |
| Return to your point | "Going back to what I was saying..." |
| Summarise | "So, to sum up, the next step is..." |
| Close kindly | "Is there anything else you would like to ask me?" |

## A short practice routine for your shifts

You do not need an hour. Ten minutes on four or five days is more useful than one long session.

1. **Day one:** Read the card above aloud twice. Record yourself on your phone and listen to your voice, not your grammar.
2. **Day two:** Practise the four steps (stop, acknowledge, answer, return) with five short interruptions that a colleague or friend invents.
3. **Day three:** Choose three phrases from the table and use each one in a real conversation at work or at home, even if the situation is small.
4. **Day four:** Write your own card, with a different setting and a different worry, and ask a partner to interrupt on the second or third line.
5. **Day five:** Listen to the day one recording again. Note one thing that has improved and one to work on next week.

Keep every card fictional. Never use a real patient's name, details or story in your practice, even if you change a few words.
