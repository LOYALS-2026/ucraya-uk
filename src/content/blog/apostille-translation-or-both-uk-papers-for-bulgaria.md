An apostille and a translation do different jobs, and many Bulgarian offices ask for both. The apostille is a stamped certificate on the UK paper that vouches for the signature, seal or stamp. The translation puts the words into Bulgarian. Ask the receiving Bulgarian office exactly what it wants before you pay for either.

## What an apostille proves and what it does not

An apostille comes from the Hague Apostille Convention of 1961 (the Convention Abolishing the Requirement of Legalisation for Foreign Public Documents). According to the Hague Conference page we read on 21 November 2026, it replaces a longer legalisation process with a single certificate issued where the document comes from. The page also says only the designated authority of each country can issue or check one.

In the UK, the authority is the Legalisation Office, which belongs to the Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office (FCDO). The [gov.uk page on legalising documents](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised) says the office checks whether the signatures, stamps or seals on your document match its own records. If they do, it attaches the apostille.

That tells you what an apostille is not. It does not say the content of your document is true. It does not translate anything. It does not tell a Bulgarian office to accept the paper. It only confirms that the signature, seal or stamp is genuine.

The service cannot legalise documents issued outside the UK.

## What the translation adds

If the Bulgarian office reads the document, it needs it in Bulgarian. The translation is the part that makes the words usable, and the apostille is the part that makes the stamp usable.

The Bulgarian Ministry of Foreign Affairs page on civil status, which we read on 21 November 2026, describes the rule for civil papers such as marriage and birth records. Where the paper is brought straight to a municipality in Bulgaria, it is to come with a certified translation into Bulgarian. The page says this is made by a Bulgarian diplomatic or consular mission, or by a translation company that has a contract with the Ministry. It also says the signature of the Bulgarian translator is certified by a Bulgarian notary.

## The usual order: apostille first

For a UK paper going to Bulgaria, plan the apostille first. The apostille is attached to the UK document itself, or to a copy certified by a UK notary or solicitor, and the Ministry page speaks of the legalised document being accompanied by a certified translation. Ask the receiving office whether the translation should cover the apostille as well.

The Ministry page, for civil status papers, gives the order in two steps: a document from a state without a legal assistance treaty that waives legalisation is to be legalised by an apostille under the Hague Convention. It also says a document from a state that does have such a treaty does not need legalising. We have not read anything on that page, or on gov.uk, that tells us which group the UK falls into, so we do not say. Ask the receiving Bulgarian office whether it wants an apostille on your UK paper.

We read nothing on either page about translating before or after the apostille, so do not assume an order. Check it with the office before you order the translation.

## FCDO services, fees and times

The gov.uk page lists the options and says you apply for all of them online. We read the page on 21 November 2026, and fees and times can change.

- Standard paper service: [£45 per document, plus courier or postage costs, usually up to 25 working days plus courier or postage time](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised).
- e-Apostille: [£35 per document, up to 2 working days](https://www.gov.uk/get-document-legalised), for documents that are eligible. You upload PDF files signed electronically by a UK notary or solicitor.

The page says these services may take longer, for example if a signature needs to be checked, and that the fee is not returned if your document cannot be legalised.

The e-Apostille matters here, because the Hague Conference page says an e-Apostille cannot be refused only because it is electronic. That is the Convention's rule, and a Bulgarian office may still tell you what it wants, so ask whether a paper apostille is needed.

## Papers the FCDO will not apostille as they are

The gov.uk page says the Legalisation Office can legalise a document if it was issued by a court and sealed with a wet ink court seal, by a public registry (such as a birth, marriage or death certificate, or a Companies House company certificate), by a government department and signed by an official, or by a registered doctor. Other documents can be legalised if a UK public official such as a notary or solicitor has certified them. The page gives a power of attorney, a contract, a qualification certificate and copies of a passport as examples, and says the list is not complete.

So a plain photocopy you made yourself is not on the list. Neither is a degree certificate on its own: the page lists a qualification certificate certified by a public official.

Three more points from the page. You cannot get an e-Apostille for birth, death, marriage, civil partnership or adoption certificates, or other General Register Office documents, so those need the paper service. The page also lists police and disclosure certificates as not eligible for an e-Apostille. And you should check with the person asking for the document whether they need the original or a certified copy.
